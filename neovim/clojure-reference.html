Clojure editing and evaluation in Neovim

The common keybindings exposed by your Conjure profile: evaluate Clojure through nREPL, inspect results, navigate source, and edit s-expressions structurally with nvim-paredit.

Local leader: \ Conjure + nvim-paredit Verified against loaded profile 10 July 2026

Orientation

Nearly every Conjure mapping begins with your local leader: a literal backslash.

How to read \ee

In Normal mode, press \, then e, then e. These are sequential keys, not a simultaneous chord.

Your profile deliberately disables automatic REPL startup and Conjure’s automatic omnifunc ownership. Start the project REPL using your normal project workflow, then use \cf to connect through a port file. Your separate LSP/omnifunc completion remains untouched; Ctrl-\ then Ctrl-R explicitly asks Conjure for nREPL completion.

The essential workflow

If you remember only one strip of keys, make it this one.

\cf

Connect to the nREPL advertised by .nrepl-port or another configured port file.

\ee

Evaluate the innermost form beneath the cursor.

\er

Evaluate the outermost, or root, form beneath the cursor—usually the complete defn, def, or test.

\eb

Evaluate the current in-memory buffer, including unsaved edits.

\lg

Toggle the Conjure log to inspect results and output.

\gK

Show documentation for the symbol under the cursor.

Evaluation

“Current form” means the innermost enclosing pair. “Root form” means the outermost enclosing pair.

Current versus root

  (reduce + (map :price xs)))

└────────────────────────────┘  \er anywhere inside evaluates the whole defn

Structural editing with nvim-paredit

Your profile uses mini.pairs to insert balanced delimiters and nvim-paredit to reshape them. For slurp and barf, the first character says which way a delimiter moves; the second character names that delimiter.

Balanced insertion: in Clojure buffers, typing (, [, {, or " automatically inserts its matching closing character. This comes from mini.pairs; Paredit then lets you move those boundaries structurally.
>move right· <move left· )closing edge· (opening edge
KeysCommandEffect
>)Forward slurpMove ) right and absorb the next expression.
<)Forward barfMove ) left and eject the final expression.
<(Backward slurpMove ( left and absorb the previous expression.
>(Backward barfMove ( right and eject the first expression.
\@Splice formRemove the enclosing delimiters while retaining their contents.
\oRaise formReplace the enclosing form with the current nested form.
\ORaise elementReplace the enclosing form with the current element.
>e / <eDrag elementMove the current element right or left among siblings.
>p / <pDrag pairMove a key/value-style pair right or left.
>f / <fDrag formMove the current delimited form right or left within its parent.

\@ uses the conventional Paredit name “splice”; if you look for it in nvim-paredit’s API, the function is named unwrap_form_under_cursor.

Slurp and barf

;; >) forward slurp
(map inc xs) more
(map inc xs more)

;; <) forward barf
(map inc xs more)
(map inc xs) more

Splice versus raise

(+ 1 (* 2 3) 4)

;; \@ inside (* 2 3)
(+ 1 * 2 3 4)

;; \o inside (* 2 3)
(* 2 3)

A gentle practice path

  1. Connect with \cf, then evaluate simple expressions with \ee.
  2. Place the cursor deep inside a defn and compare \ee with \er.
  3. Keep \lg, \ll, and \ve nearby while learning how output and failures appear.
  4. Learn forward slurp/barf—>) and <)—then add their backward counterparts.
  5. Use \@ and \o with u nearby until splice versus raise becomes instinctive.
  6. Add \gK, \gd, and explicit Conjure completion when exploring unfamiliar APIs.
